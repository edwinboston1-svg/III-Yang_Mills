THE GROUND  
Yang–Mills Theory after the Alpöge–Sussman Manuscripts 
What Is Constructed, What Is Claimed, and the Object That Remains 
 
Edwin Boston III 
28 October 2026 · revised 2 November 2026 
Abstract 
A proof that quantum Yang–Mills theory has a mass gap must stand on a construction of the 
theory, and the most complete public construction now in view is the corpus of manuscripts 
that Levent Alpöge and Ethan Sussman have posted since 6 October 2026, carrying Bałaban's 
renormalisation group from the lattice to the continuum for the gauge group SU(2). This paper 
fixes that ground. It reads the corpus from its primary sources and proves, clause by clause 
and with exact references, what the corpus asserts and at what strength. In three dimensions 
an 854-page manuscript asserts continuum Schwinger functions of the plaquette-energy 
density, the lattice form of tr F², which satisfy the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms in the 
clustering components of infinite-volume limits taken along subsequences, together with a 
short-distance law that makes the theory non-trivial; it asserts no mass gap. In four 
dimensions a 153-page draft deduces, from inputs it declares clause by clause and from 
supporting statements two of which are not yet written, continuum limits on ℝ⁴ along 
subsequences with regularity, reflection positivity, symmetry and hypercubic invariance; its 
authors call the underlying argument extremely long and not understandable as it stands, and 
state that the corpus has not been satisfactorily vetted. The paper then proves that, for a 
reflection-positive theory, a mass gap is the same property as exponential clustering of the 
reflection-positive correlations, and that a lower bound on the lattice mass gap, uniform in 
physical units along the lattice sequence of such a construction, supplies at once the three 
properties the corpus withholds: a unique vacuum, the cluster property and a mass gap. It

1.  Introduction 
The quantum Yang–Mills problem of the Clay Mathematics Institute is a single sentence. For every 
compact simple gauge group G it asks for a proof that a non-trivial quantum Yang–Mills theory exists 
on ℝ⁴ and has a mass gap Δ > 0.1 The sentence joins two demands of different kinds. The first is a 
construction: a system of correlation functions, free of every cutoff, defined on the whole of space-
time and obeying axioms at least as strong as those of Wightman and of Osterwalder and Schrader. 
The second is an inequality: the spectrum of the Hamiltonian of the theory so constructed must leave 
an interval above zero empty. The inequality cannot be stated before the construction exists, and 
when the construction proceeds by compactness, the problem's own text, in a footnote examined in 
Section 3, does not count it as complete until properties of this kind are proved for the limit. 
In two dimensions the continuum Yang–Mills measure was constructed in 1989, by Driver and 
by Gross, King and Sengupta.2 In three dimensions Bałaban controlled Wilson's lattice theory on a 
torus by a renormalisation group whose bounds are uniform in the lattice spacing; in four dimensions 
he proved the same bounds under the hypothesis that the running couplings stay below a constant 
at every scale; the discharge of that hypothesis, his Theorem 2, was announced in 1987 and never 
published, and it is a theorem on this work's path (§8.3).3 When the problem was posed, its authors 
wrote that a detailed construction on a compact space would be a major breakthrough, and that even 
then "no present ideas point the direction to establish the existence of a mass gap that is uniform in 
the volume. Nor do present methods suggest how to obtain the existence of the infinite volume limit 
T⁴ → ℝ⁴."4 
On 6 October 2026 Ethan Sussman opened a public repository for the joint work of Levent Alpöge 
and himself, whose stated aim is "to induce LLMs to develop, push, and extend Balaban's program to 
construct Yang--Mills_4 (YM4) and other quantum gauge theories".5 It now holds an 854-page 
manuscript on three-dimensional SU(2) Yang–Mills theory, a revised edition of 856 pages, a 1,044-
page sequel on curvature bilinears, a 263-page manuscript on three-dimensional quantum 
electrodynamics, a 30-page manuscript on rotation invariance from reflection positivity, and a 153-
page zeroth draft on four-dimensional SU(2) Yang–Mills theory.6 Peter Woit discussed the repository

Sussman at its commit of 27 October 2026. By the problem statement we mean the official description 
of Jaffe and Witten. Throughout, a denotes a lattice spacing and b > 0 the physical inverse coupling of 
the three-dimensional theory, with bare inverse coupling β = b/a.

positivity and symmetry, whence a Hilbert space with a vacuum and a non-negative Hamiltonian;

3.  What the Problem Demands 
3.1  Euclidean fields and the axioms of Osterwalder and Schrader 
A Euclidean quantum field theory in d dimensions is given by its Schwinger functions Sn, n ≥ 1: 
continuous linear functionals on test functions of n points of ℝd. For the composite fields from which 
a gauge theory is built, the natural test functions vanish, with all their derivatives, wherever two 
points coincide, and the corpus works with exactly these.8 Let θ be the reflection xd ↦ −xd, let τv be 
translation by v, and let 𝒮₊ consist of the test functions supported where every point has positive last 
coordinate. A finite family is a sequence f = (f₀, f₁, …, fN) with fn ∈ 𝒮₊(ℝdn); it stands for a polynomial in 
the smeared field, and we write

This is the theorem of Osterwalder and Schrader.10 Reflection positivity is the axiom that 
manufactures the Hilbert space, and with it the Hamiltonian whose spectrum the mass gap concerns. 
3.2  The mass gap 
The problem statement defines the gap and states the problem in these words:11 
"A quantum field theory has a mass gap Δ if H has no spectrum in the interval (0, Δ) for some 
Δ > 0. The supremum of such Δ is the mass m, and we require m < ∞. 
Yang–Mills Existence and Mass Gap. Prove that for any compact simple gauge group G, a non-
trivial quantum Yang–Mills theory exists on ℝ⁴ and has a mass gap Δ > 0. Existence includes 
establishing axiomatic properties at least as strong as those cited in [45, 35]." 
The two references are Streater and Wightman's axioms and those of Osterwalder and Schrader. The 
text adds that clustering is "an important consequence of the existence of a mass gap": connected 
vacuum correlations of local operators decay exponentially in the distance. It also records that "the 
existence of a uniform gap for finite-volume approximations may play a fundamental role in the proof 
of existence of the infinite-volume limit."12 Section 7 proves the precise form of both remarks that the 
present ground requires.

3.3  Non-triviality 
The problem fixes what it means for the constructed theory to be Yang–Mills theory and not some 
other theory:13 
"To establish existence of four-dimensional quantum gauge theory with gauge group G, one 
should define a quantum field theory (in the above sense) with local quantum field operators 
in correspondence with the gauge-invariant local polynomials in the curvature F and its 
covariant derivatives, such as Tr FijFkl(x). Correlation functions of the quantum field operators 
should agree at short distances with the predictions of asymptotic freedom and perturbative 
renormalization theory, as described in textbooks." 
Two features of this demand govern the reading of the corpus: the theory must carry all the gauge-
invariant local polynomials in the curvature, and its short-distance behaviour must be that of 
asymptotic freedom. 
3.4  Limits obtained by compactness 
A construction often proves bounds uniform in the cutoffs, extracts a convergent subsequence by 
compactness, and calls the limit the theory. The problem statement addresses this practice in its 
second footnote:14 
"We specifically exclude weak-existence (compactness) as the solution to the existence part 
of the Millennium problem, unless one also uses other techniques to establish properties of 
the limit (such as the existence of a mass gap and the axioms)." 
Proposition 3.2 (The standard for a limit by compactness). A system of Schwinger functions 
obtained as a limit point of cutoff theories by compactness establishes the existence part of the problem 
only together with a proof of properties of that limit, and the problem statement names the mass gap 
and the axioms as such properties. 
Proof. This is the content of the footnote quoted above, read at its literal strength. The footnote does 
not require the limit to be unique; it requires the properties to be proved for the limit that has been 
obtained. ∎

4.2  Reflection positivity and the transfer matrix 
Osterwalder and Seiler proved that Wilson's measure is reflection positive: if ϑ is the reflection of Λ 
in a lattice hyperplane orthogonal to a coordinate axis and F is a function of the bond variables on 
one side of it, then ∫ ϑF̄ · F dμβ ≥ 0.17 The problem statement calls this "a major advantage" and adds 
that "establishing a quantum mechanical Hilbert space is part of the solution to this Millennium 
problem."18 Reflection positivity makes the lattice theory a quantum mechanics. Lüscher constructed 
for Wilson's action a self-adjoint, strictly positive transfer matrix T acting on the gauge-invariant 
functions of the bond variables of one time slice, so that time-ordered correlations on a torus of time 
extent Na are traces of products of T with the observables.19 On a torus of finite spatial volume T is 
an integral operator with a continuous, strictly positive kernel on a compact space. By Jentzsch's 
theorem its largest eigenvalue λ₀ is simple, with a strictly positive eigenfunction ψ₀, and every other 
eigenvalue is strictly smaller.20 
Definition 4.1 (The lattice gap). Let λ₁ be the largest eigenvalue of T below λ₀ on the gauge-invariant 
sector. The lattice mass gap is mlat = log(λ₀/λ₁), in units of the lattice spacing; its value in physical units 
is mlat/a. 
In the state defined by ψ₀, which is the limit of infinite time extent at fixed spatial volume, an

A inserted, a vector ΦA on the time slice, and ⟨ϑĀ · τnA⟩₀ = ⟨ΦA, (T/λ₀)n ΦA⟩ for every number n of time 
steps. This is the lattice form of Theorem 3.1.

4.3  The renormalisation group of Bałaban 
A continuum limit requires bounds on the lattice theory that are uniform as the spacing tends to zero 
along a sequence of bare couplings tuned by asymptotic freedom. Bałaban's renormalisation group 
provides such bounds on a torus. It integrates out the fluctuations of one length scale after another, 
each step followed by a rescaling, chooses gauges that vary from cell to cell and from scale to scale, 
and controls the effective actions uniformly in the spacing, with an analysis of asymptotic freedom 
and of the regions of large field.22 The problem statement recorded that these estimates "need to be 
extended to the study of expectations of gauge-invariant functions of the fields."23 The corpus is an 
attempt to carry out that extension and then to pass to infinite volume. 
5.  The Three-Dimensional Manuscripts 
This section proves part (i) of the Ground Theorem. 
5.1  The documents 
The three-dimensional work consists of two manuscripts. The first, YMM1, is titled "Scalar fields from 
three-dimensional lattice Yang–Mills theory", carries no author or date line, and runs to 854 pages.24 
A revised edition, posted on 27 October 2026, runs to 856 pages. Its change list records twenty-four 
corrections and states: "The statements of the principal Theorems 0 and A–C are unchanged."25 The 
second manuscript, YMM2, is titled "Curvature observables, dynamics, and conditional confinement

7.2  The gap is exponential clustering 
The decisive observation is that, for a reflection-positive theory, a mass gap and exponential decay of 
the reflection-positive correlations are one and the same property. The argument is the spectral 
theorem applied to the semigroup of Osterwalder and Schrader; its weight here lies in the 
identification it makes. Let S be reflection positive and invariant under time translations, and let H, Ω 
and H ≥ 0 be the Hilbert space, the vacuum and the Hamiltonian that Osterwalder and Schrader 
construct from these two properties, with the relations of Theorem 3.1. This is exactly the structure 
that both manuscripts assert for their limits.

8.  The Ground Handed Over 
8.1  The object that remains 
The object that remains is the infinite-volume mass gap of four-dimensional SU(2) lattice Yang–Mills 
theory at every coupling, uniform in physical units along the scaling sequence: a constant m > 0 with 
mlat(j) ≥ m aj at every rung of the construction's sequence, a lower bound on the lattice gap in physical 
units, uniform in the spacing and in the volume. By Theorem 7.4 it is the mass gap of every continuum 
limit point, and the same object for every compact simple gauge group completes the problem's 
demand for a gap. Two features of this inequality fix the work it demands.

The first is its scale. In lattice units the required gap tends to zero with the spacing, like m aj, so it 
cannot be read at a single coupling. The lattice gaps that are proved directly hold at strong coupling, 
where the transfer matrix mixes rapidly: for SU(2) this is the theorem of Osterwalder and Seiler, and 
for every finite gauge group it is among the theorems checked by machine in §8.2.59 For SU(2) the 
constant of that gap is a theorem of Edwin Boston III: the conditional law of one link given its six 
staples, the heat-bath kernel, moves in total variation by at most 2β/(3π) times the change of the 
staple sum, because the mean absolute deviation of a coordinate of a von Mises–Fisher law on the 
three-sphere is at most its value 4/(3π) at the uniform law, with equality there alone; so Dobrushin's 
single-site condition holds on the full lattice for β < 3π/72 = 0.1309, and in the temporal gauge on 
lattices open in time, where every time link is the identity and a space link has fourteen neighbouring 
links rather than eighteen, four spatial plaquettes with three other space links each and two temporal 
plaquettes with one, for β < 3π/56 = 0.1683.60 The construction's sequence runs into the scaling 
window of weak bare coupling, where asymptotic freedom ties the bare coupling to the spacing. The 
inequality must be carried from the one end of the sequence to the other. 
The second is its carrier. The carrier is the renormalisation group itself: a chain of blocking steps 
from a fixed physical scale, at which the effective coupling is small and Bałaban's estimates hold, 
down to the scale of the gap, with the error of every step certified. Five links compose this carrier, 
and each has its status. The first is the ultraviolet half: the blocking steps from the cutoff to a fixed 
physical scale at which the effective coupling is still small, with the effective action held near Wilson's 
uniformly in the number of steps. This is Bałaban's theorem, and what the carrier needs of it beyond 
his published estimates is stated as an interface: the convergence of the correlation functions of the 
effective theory at that scale as the cutoff is removed, and the Euclidean invariance of the limit, the 
two pieces the zeroth draft deduces conditionally and names among its inputs. The second is a single 
bound at the crossover scale, where the effective coupling ceases to be small. Along the Gaussian 
scales above it the mixing coefficient of a fibre is exactly one, that of a massless Gaussian, so no 
contraction is to be certified there; the content lies at the crossover scale alone, and the object there 
is the influence functional of the blocked action: the covariance of a boundary-adjacent link's 
projection with every plaquette of the block under the worst boundary datum, equivalently the mixed 
second differences of the non-Wilson part of the renormalised action. This bound is the open 
mathematics of the route. The third is the infrared half, the gap of the blocked theory inside the 
strong-coupling disk, which is the theorem of Osterwalder and Seiler for SU(2) and a theorem 
checked by machine for every finite gauge group. The fourth is the uniformity of the second link's 
bound in the cutoff: the effective actions at the fixed scale differ across the sequence by tilts of small

independent of the cutoff, which is the inequality of Theorem 7.4 with one constant. The fifth is the

The passage from the second link to the gap is itself a theorem, and this section proves it at the

8.4  Coda 
The ground, then, is this. A construction of the corpus's kind, as its manuscripts state it, delivers a 
limit that is controlled at short distances and silent at long ones: a theory whose correlations obey 
the axioms of positivity and symmetry, whose coupling is seen to run, and whose large-distance 
behaviour is not controlled. Theorem 7.1 shows that the missing control is a single spectral fact. 
Theorem 7.4 shows that this fact is a property of the rungs: an inequality on the lattice, where 
reflection positivity has made a quantum mechanics of the theory before any limit is taken. The proof 
that follows takes up that inequality on that sequence.
